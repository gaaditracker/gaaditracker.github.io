If you own a car on Uber or Ola and someone else drives it, every evening ends with the same sum: how much of today’s money is the driver’s, and how much is yours. Getting that sum right, and getting both of you to agree on it before the first shift, saves most of the arguments that end owner–driver arrangements.

This guide works through the numbers with one example day throughout.

## The example day

Ravi drives TN 23 TK 4521, a CNG sedan, on Uber.

| | |
|---|---|
| Km driven | 182 km |
| Uber earnings for the day | ₹3,450 |
| CNG filled | ₹1,100 (12.8 kg) |

“Earnings” here means what the platform pays out for the day’s trips, after the platform’s own commission. That is the money that actually reaches the owner–driver pair, so it is what the split should be based on.

## Before you calculate: settle three questions

Most disputes aren’t about maths. They come from three things nobody agreed on.

1. **Is the split on revenue, or on revenue minus fuel?** A 30% share of ₹3,450 is ₹1,035. A 30% share of ₹3,450 − ₹1,100 = ₹2,350 is ₹705. That is a ₹330 difference every day.
2. **Who pays for fuel during the shift?** If the driver pays at the pump from the day’s cash, that cash has to be accounted for.
3. **How are advances settled?** A ₹2,000 advance for a puncture is owner money. Agree that it comes off the next settlement, not “sometime”.

Write the answers down. In Gaadi Tracker the rule you set and every advance you record are visible to the driver in their own app, which does the writing down for you.

## Model 1: Percentage of revenue

The driver gets a fixed share of what the car earned.

**Driver’s share = revenue × percentage**

At 30%: ₹3,450 × 0.30 = **₹1,035**. The owner has ₹2,415, out of which fuel and the car’s other running costs are paid. After ₹1,100 of CNG, the owner is left with ₹1,315 for the day.

Percentage is the most common model for platform cabs because both sides share a good day and a slow one. Typical shares vary a lot by city and by whether the driver pays fuel, so don’t copy a number from someone else’s fleet without also copying their fuel arrangement.

## Model 2: Slab

A slab pays a lower percentage on slow days and a higher one on good days. You set a daily revenue line, a low rate and a high rate. The whole day’s revenue is paid at one rate, depending on which side of the line the day lands.

With a ₹3,000 slab, 25% below and 32% at or above:

| Day’s revenue | Rate | Driver’s share |
|---|---|---|
| ₹2,800 | 25% | ₹700 |
| ₹3,000 | 32% | ₹960 |
| ₹3,450 | 32% | ₹1,104 |

Look at the jump at the line: ₹2,800 pays ₹700, ₹3,000 pays ₹960. That jump is the point. It gives the driver a clear reason to take the two extra trips that cross the slab. Set the line near a good-but-reachable day for that car, not at the best day you’ve ever seen.

## Model 3: Daily rent

The driver pays the owner a fixed amount and keeps the rest.

**Driver keeps = revenue − rent**

With ₹1,800 rent on the ₹3,450 day, the owner gets ₹1,800 and Ravi keeps **₹1,650**. On a ₹2,200 day, the owner still gets ₹1,800 and Ravi keeps ₹400.

Rent moves the risk of a slow day onto the driver. It suits experienced drivers who run their own hours, and owners who would rather have a steady figure than watch daily earnings. It needs a clear agreement on fuel, because a driver on rent will usually expect to buy fuel themselves.

## Model 4: Monthly salary

A fixed monthly amount, whatever the car earns. To compare it with the other models, turn it into a cost per working day. Drivers usually work around 26 days a month, so:

**Cost per shift = monthly salary ÷ 26**

₹18,200 a month is **₹700 a shift**. On the ₹3,450 day that is cheaper for the owner than 30% commission. On a ₹2,000 day it is dearer. Salary suits owners who want to control the driver’s hours and keep the earnings risk themselves.

## The same day, four ways

| Model | Driver gets | Owner keeps before fuel |
|---|---|---|
| 30% of revenue | ₹1,035 | ₹2,415 |
| Slab ₹3,000, 25% / 32% | ₹1,104 | ₹2,346 |
| ₹1,800 daily rent | ₹1,650 | ₹1,800 |
| ₹18,200 salary | ₹700 | ₹2,750 |

Change the day’s revenue and the order changes. Try your own numbers in the [driver pay calculator](/driver-salary-commission-calculator).

## Cash in hand is a separate sum

The driver’s share is one number. The cash the driver is holding is another. On the example day Ravi collected ₹3,450 and spent ₹1,100 on CNG, so he is holding ₹2,350 of the day’s money. If you had also given him a ₹2,000 advance and he had paid you ₹27,500 so far this month, you need a running total to know where you stand.

Gaadi Tracker keeps that running total for each driver: revenue minus expenses, plus advances, minus payments to the owner. The driver sees it as “Balance to pay owner” or “Owner owes you”, and you see the same figure. Settle against that, not against memory.

## When you change the rule

Rates change: a new driver proves themselves, fuel prices move, a slab turns out to be set too high. When you change a driver’s rule in Gaadi Tracker, it applies from the next shift and every earlier shift keeps the rule it was recorded with. Past hisab doesn’t move unless you choose a back-dated start.

## Quick checklist

- Base the split on platform payouts, not on trip fares before platform commission.
- Agree in writing whether fuel comes out before the split.
- Record every advance on the day it is given.
- Settle against a running balance, not the last WhatsApp message.
- Review the rate after a month of real numbers, not after one bad day.
