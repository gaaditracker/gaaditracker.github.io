Every owner who has run more than one vehicle has had the feeling: the fuel spend went up, the km didn’t. Sometimes it is a tired engine or a bad month of traffic. Sometimes it is a ₹600 fill reported as ₹900.

You can’t stand at every pump. You don’t need to. Fuel padding shows up in arithmetic, and the arithmetic only needs two numbers you should be recording anyway: **km driven** and **fuel filled**.

## The one number that catches it: fuel average

Fuel average is km driven divided by fuel used.

**Fuel average = km driven ÷ litres (or kg of CNG)**

A lorry that ran 7,420 km on 1,810 litres of diesel averaged 7,420 ÷ 1,810 = **4.1 km per litre**.

The exact figure matters less than how steady it is. A given vehicle, on its usual routes, with its usual load, gives a fairly steady average month to month. When a bill is padded, the litres go up on paper while the km stay the same, so the average drops.

### A worked example

Say your CNG cab has averaged about 22 km per kg for months. This week:

| | |
|---|---|
| Km driven (odometer) | 1,140 km |
| CNG entered | 66 kg |
| Average | 1,140 ÷ 66 = 17.3 km/kg |

That is more than 20% below normal. At 22 km/kg, 1,140 km needs about 52 kg. Fourteen extra kg is roughly ₹1,200 at around ₹86 a kg. Before you accuse anyone, check the boring causes (below). If none of them apply, you have a conversation to have.

## Rule out the honest reasons first

A falling average is a question, not a verdict. Check these before talking to the driver:

- **Load and route.** A lorry carrying heavier loads, or a cab doing more city traffic than highway, uses more fuel per km.
- **Tyres and service.** Low tyre pressure, a clogged air filter or an overdue service all hurt the average. If service is overdue, do that first.
- **AC season.** Summer months cost cabs real fuel.
- **Idling.** Long waits at airports or loading points with the engine running.
- **A bad odometer entry.** If the km are wrong, the average is wrong. This is why the odometer reading has to be checked against the last one.

If the average is steady and then drops in a week when none of these changed, that is the week to look at.

## Fill sizes that can’t be true

The second check is simpler. Every tank and cylinder has a capacity. A cab’s CNG cylinder holds a fixed amount, a lorry’s diesel tank holds a fixed number of litres. A single fill larger than the tank can take, or two big fills a few km apart, is worth a question on its own.

Compare each fill with the km since the last one too. A full tank of diesel after 90 km on a lorry that does 4 km per litre doesn’t add up: it only burned about 22 litres.

## Habits that make padding pointless

Catching padding is good. Making it not worth trying is better.

1. **Odometer reading at the start and end of every shift.** Without real km, no average means anything. Readings that can’t go backwards are the foundation.
2. **Quantity, not just amount.** Ask for litres or kg on every fill, not just the rupee figure. A bill for ₹1,100 is hard to check; 12.8 kg at the pump’s price is easy.
3. **Record the fill when it happens.** A fill entered the same minute, on the driver’s phone, is harder to round up than one remembered at night.
4. **Pay the pump directly where you can.** A fuel card or a UPI payment from your own account leaves a record that matches the pump’s.
5. **Look at the average every week.** Not every bill. Just the average per vehicle. It takes a minute and it is where padding shows.
6. **Tell drivers you look.** Most padding stops the day a driver knows the owner checks the average.

## Summary

- Fuel padding shows up as a falling fuel average.
- Know each vehicle’s normal average, and look at it weekly.
- Rule out load, route, service and season before suspecting anyone.
- Record quantity on every fill, at the moment of filling.
- Real odometer readings are the base of all of it.
